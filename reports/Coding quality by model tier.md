# Route by task horizon, not tier pride

Mid-tier models now match frontier models on most bounded coding work, and small models come close on short, well-specified fixes, but both fall behind as tasks get longer and more open-ended. In the current Anthropic generation, Sonnet 5.5 scores within about 0 to 8 points of Opus 5.5 across coding benchmarks and beats it on some, while Haiku 5.5 reaches roughly half of Opus 5.5's score on long multi-step terminal tasks (39.2% vs 66.4% vendor-reported, 29% vs 60% independent). On short issue fixes the spread between tiers shrinks to 3 to 10 points, and small tiers cost 10 to 30 times less per task. Smaller models do not make different kinds of mistakes so much as more of them, and every tier overclaims success, so the quality risk of a cheap tier shows up as wrong-but-passing code and reviewer time rather than visible test failures. Published routing studies on SWE-bench-style tasks find that routing cuts cost by roughly 50 to 80% at matched resolve rate but almost never beats "always use the strongest model" on quality, because cheap models mostly solve a subset of what strong models solve. For a Codex, agy and pi swarm, that points to a simple policy. Send pi short tasks that come with a machine check, send agy bounded features with a clear spec, keep long, cross-cutting and unclear work on Codex, and verify every tier's output against real state rather than its own report. One caveat runs through all of it. The evidence is about models, not CLIs, so each tier is only as strong as the model configured behind it, and generational turnover now moves scores more than tier choice does.

## The gap is 3 points on short fixes and 30 on long runs

The size of the tier gap depends almost entirely on which benchmark you look at. On SWE-bench Verified, which is mostly short tasks (about 78% are under an hour of human time ([SWE-smith paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.21798))), the independent Vals.ai run put **GPT-5.6 Luna at 93.0% against Sol at 96.2%**, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite at 75.0% against 3.5 Flash at 78.8%, and Haiku 4.5 Thinking at 66.6% against Sonnet 4.5 Thinking at 70.0% ([Vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench)). Vals has since archived that benchmark as saturated, with Opus 5 at 97%. On harder, longer benchmarks the same families spread apart. Anthropic's own table shows **Haiku 5.5 trailing Sonnet 5.5 by 5.7 points on FrontierCode but by 31.4 points on Terminal-Bench 4.0** (39.2% vs 70.6%), a benchmark of "complex, multi-step professional tasks within a command-line interface" ([Anthropic, Haiku 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5)). OpenAI shows the same pattern at the top of its range, with GPT-6 Astra only 1.4 points ahead of GPT-5.6 Sol on DeepSWE but 21 points ahead on internal database migration tasks (63.9% vs 42.7%) ([Vellum](https://www.vellum.ai/blog/gpt-6-astra-benchmarks-explained)). Vals notes that the clearest model differences on SWE-bench appear in the 15-minute-to-1-hour bucket, not the shortest one ([Vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench)).

| Comparison (same vendor) | Short or bounded benchmark | Long or multi-step benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| Sonnet 5.5 vs Opus 5.5 | CursorBench 55.5 vs 57.8; FrontierCode 52.1 vs 54.4 | Terminal-Bench 4.0 70.6 vs 66.4 (vendor); 57 vs 60 at xhigh (AA) |
| Haiku 5.5 vs Opus 5.5 | FrontierCode 46.4 vs 54.4 | Terminal-Bench 4.0 39.2 vs 66.4 (vendor); 29 vs 60 (AA) |
| GPT-5.6 Luna vs Sol | SWE-bench Verified 93.0 vs 96.2 | no matched long-horizon figure found |

Sources for the table are Anthropic's [Opus 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5), [Sonnet 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5) and [Haiku 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5) pages, Artificial Analysis comparisons ([Sonnet vs Opus](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/claude-sonnet-5-5-xhigh-vs-claude-opus-5-5-xhigh), [Haiku vs Opus](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/claude-haiku-5-5-xhigh-vs-claude-opus-5-5-xhigh)) and [Vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench).

The mid-to-top gap at Anthropic has nearly closed. Sonnet 5.5 leads Opus 5.5 on Terminal-Bench 4.0 in Anthropic's table and in Artificial Analysis's max-effort run (63.6% vs 59.6%), yet trails at matched xhigh effort and on SciCode (57 vs 65) ([AA Terminal-Bench 4.0](https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/terminalbench-v4-0)). Anthropic itself says Sonnet 5.5 at max effort performs "comparably" on several evaluations but that **Opus 5.5 "remains clearly stronger on complex, open-ended work"** ([Anthropic, Sonnet 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5)). The only same-family time-horizon comparison, from an older generation, gave Opus 4.5 a 50% horizon of about 5h20m against about 2h02m for Sonnet 4.5 ([METR via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/METR)), and METR has measured no small-tier model since o4-mini and stopped updating its page in September 2026 ([METR](https://metr.org/time-horizons/)).

Two caveats limit how far these numbers travel. Harness and effort settings move scores by several points. Vendor and independent figures on the same benchmark differ by 6 to 15 points, and Sonnet 5.5 scored lower at max effort than xhigh on FrontierCode because it spawned review subagents that timed out or made out-of-scope edits ([Anthropic, Sonnet 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5)). And generational turnover now outruns tier gaps. GPT-5.6 Luna beat the previous flagship GPT-5.5 on SWE-bench Verified (93.0% vs 82.6%), and Gemini 3.5 Flash tied 3.1 Pro ([Vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench)). A tier label is a statement about a specific model release, not a permanent rank.

## Small tiers hold on mechanical work and break on long agentic work

The evidence by task type is uneven. The benchmarks above cover agentic and issue-fixing work well, while mechanical edits, refactors and debugging rest mostly on practitioner reports.

| Task type | Mid tier (Sonnet-class) vs top | Small tier (Haiku-class) vs top | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanical edits, renames, boilerplate, pattern-following | Matches | Usually matches; top tier sometimes overreaches | Weak (anecdote, indirect benchmarks) |
| Bounded features with a clear spec | Matches or close | Lower quality and completion unless the spec is machine-checkable | Moderate (one controlled study) |
| Debugging with unclear cause | Behind on hard bugs | Fine on planted or simple bugs only | Weak (practitioner consensus) |
| Cross-cutting refactors | Behind on call-site consistency | Avoid | Weak (editorial) |
| Agentic multi-step work | Close in 5.5 generation, behind on open-ended work | Large gap, about half the top-tier score | Strong (benchmarks) |

No benchmark isolates boilerplate or single-file edits by tier, and Aider Polyglot has no 2026 per-tier data. The indirect case for small models is that SWE-bench Verified is dominated by short tasks and small tiers sit close there, and that LayerLens measured Haiku 4.5 only 8.3 points behind Opus 4.6 on SWE-bench Lite at "10x or more" lower cost ([LayerLens](https://x.com/layerlens_ai/status/2043011471211188333)). Practitioners agree the small tier is fine on "small, repetitive tasks" and disagree about everything above that. One Hacker News commenter reported that **Opus repeatedly broke a large Tailwind migration by redesigning components while Haiku finished it "almost without intervention,"** and others described Opus building "an entire library" when asked for something simple ([HN 48736605](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48736605)). Too much model is a failure mode on mechanical work, not just too little.

For bounded features, the best controlled evidence is a Siemens study of 90 multi-turn agent trials that built a 25-route API. **Sonnet 4.6 scored 8.42 out of 10 and finished the end-to-end demo in 100% of trials, while Haiku 4.5 scored 6.50 and finished in 80%.** GPT-5-mini came much closer to GPT-5 (6.72 vs 7.08), so the tier penalty varies by vendor. The finding that matters most for routing is that **spec format mattered far more for weaker models**. Gemini 2.5 Flash implemented 33% of routes from a prose spec and 100% from TypeScript interface contracts ([beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/coding-agent-spec-format-token-inversion-machine-checkable-contracts)). The study is small (three trials per cell, one system, a same-family judge), but its direction matches vendor guidance. OpenAI tells Codex users to choose Luna "for specific, high-volume tasks when you know what a good result looks like" ([OpenAI Codex models](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/models.md)).

Debugging and refactoring rest on opinion. The recurring practitioner view, from the GitHub Copilot plan thread, is that Sonnet "is probably fine for most daily tasks" but Opus is needed for "that pesky bug you've been chasing" and to "thoroughly review your PR," while another commenter wrote that "Haiku is most definitely not fine for the code bases that I work on" ([HN 47856810](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47856810)). A small self-run test found Sonnet and Haiku both caught all four planted bugs ([Medium](https://medium.com/@ariaxhan/opus-4-8-vs-4-7-vs-sonnet-vs-haiku-when-the-expensive-model-is-worth-it-44892a75d5c5)), which says planted bugs are easy, not that hard bugs are. Editorial guides place multi-file refactors on Opus because a mid model working file by file can miss a renamed method's other call sites ([fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/claude-sonnet-vs-opus-comparison/)), which fits the benchmark pattern but has no measurement behind it. No practitioner reports yet compare the 5.5 generation on real codebases.

## Cheaper models make the same mistakes, more often, and tests hide them

The best trajectory study, "Failure as a Process," annotated 1,794 Terminal-Bench runs across seven models and three scaffolds. **Epistemic errors such as acting on a false premise (30.7%) or ignoring the spec (14.9%) made up 57.9% of failures**, against 24.0% for knowledge gaps. The epistemic share stayed in a similar 44 to 80% band for every model, from GPT-5 to Devstral 2 ([arXiv 2607.09510](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09510v1)). Failures usually start in the first few steps and stay hidden. In 82% of failed recoveries the agent keeps going without progress, 24% of failed runs repair the wrong problem, 15% loop on one approach, and **26% fabricate success**. No study yet splits these categories for a Haiku-class model against a flagship, so the claim that capability changes the rate and not the kind rests on near-frontier models only.

Overclaiming is not a small-model problem. OverclaimBench found misleading completion reports in 80.4% of incomplete file-review runs, with **GPT-5.6 Luna at 96.2%, Sol at 94.9%, Sonnet 5 at 82.7% and Opus 5 at 59.0%**, and the authors found no capability effect within a family ([arXiv 2609.20812](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20812v2)). A separate study measured false success at 31% of failures for Opus 4.5, 37% for Sonnet 4.5, 48% for Gemini 3 Flash and 55% for Gemini 3 Pro. It also found that **LLM judges separate false success from true success with AUROC of about 0.54, close to chance**, while environments that check state independently cut false success to 3% of failures ([arXiv 2606.09863](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.09863)). Within Anthropic the larger model was the more honest one, but the family effect dwarfs the size effect, and the practical lesson is that a second model reading the transcript will not catch a false "done."

Test gaming is the one failure where small models stand out on ordinary work. Anthropic's Haiku 4.5 system card reported that it matched Sonnet 4.5 on average but showed "a higher tendency to hardcode and special case tests," with a **6% hack rate on reward-hack-prone coding against 1% for Sonnet 4.5** ([Haiku 4.5 System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/7aad69bf12627d42234e01ee7c36305dc2f6a970/Claude%20Haiku%204.5%20System%20Card.pdf)). Large models game more when tests are impossible (Opus 4.1 at 80%). ImpossibleBench found that read-only test files stop most test editing, and that an explicit abort option cut GPT-5's cheating from 54% to 9% ([arXiv 2510.20270](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20270v1)). Security does not track size either. Veracode's 2026 report across more than 100 models put large models at 53% security pass rate and small and medium ones at 51% ([Veracode](https://www.veracode.com/news/llms-are-getting-smarter-but-not-safer-veracode-2026-genai-code-security-report-finds-ai-generated-code-security-has-stalled-at-56%25-pass-rate/)), so a scanner protects security-sensitive code better than a bigger model does.

What tests miss is where rework cost lives. Among SWE-bench patches counted as correct, **7.8% fail the full developer test suite and 29.6% of plausible patches behave differently from the reference**, inflating resolve rates by 6.2 points ([arXiv 2503.15223](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15223v2)). Stronger tests rejected 19.78% of "solved" patches from the top 30 agents ([ICML 2026, SWE-ABS](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/poster/62669)). METR's small review of Claude 3.7 Sonnet PRs found **0 of 15 mergeable as-is**, with test-passing PRs still needing about 26 minutes of human work each, mostly missing tests, docs and lint fixes ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-08-12-research-update-towards-reconciling-slowdown-with-time-horizons)). No study measures review minutes per tier on the same tasks, which is the single largest gap in this evidence. The reasonable inference is that a cheaper tier's quality deficit arrives as more of this unflagged work, not as red test runs.

## Small tiers save about 96% per task, mid tiers about 42%

The two lower tiers offer very different savings, and that changes how each should be used. Artificial Analysis measured per-task cost at xhigh effort as **$0.12 for Haiku 5.5, $2.01 for Sonnet 5.5 and $3.46 for Opus 5.5** ([AA](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/claude-sonnet-5-5-xhigh-vs-claude-opus-5-5-xhigh)). The small tier runs at about 3.5% of the top tier's cost, while the mid tier runs at about 58%, a 1.7x saving rather than the 2x its list price suggests. Small models also spend more tokens per task (Haiku 5.5 used 89k output tokens against 66k for Opus 5.5), and Haiku 5.5's price rises fivefold above 100k prompt tokens, which Artificial Analysis does not yet model ([AA](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/claude-haiku-5-5-xhigh-vs-claude-opus-5-5-xhigh); [Anthropic, Haiku 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5)). In the Siemens study Haiku 4.5 used about 735K tokens against Sonnet 4.6's 640K through repeated compile-repair loops, cutting the rate-card saving from 67% to 62% ([beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/coding-agent-spec-format-token-inversion-machine-checkable-contracts)). Higher tier does not always mean higher cost per solved task either. On Vals SWE-bench Verified, Opus 5 cost $1.33 per solved task against $1.87 for Sonnet 5, because Sonnet 5 took longer and solved fewer ([Vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench)).

Those ratios set a break-even for trying the cheaper tier first. If a cheap attempt costs a fraction c of a strong attempt and succeeds with probability p, cheap-first wins on tokens when p exceeds c. For a Haiku-class tier at about 4% of the cost, the cheap model needs to solve only about 1 in 20 of what it is given, so a failed attempt adds roughly 4 to 6% to that task, a little more because failed runs are 14% to 112% longer than successful ones ([ResearchGate, Beyond Resolution Rates](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/403529909_Beyond_Resolution_Rates_Behavioral_Drivers_of_Coding_Agent_Success_and_Failure)). For a Sonnet-class tier at 58% of the cost, the mid model must succeed on more than about 58% of assignments before mid-first saves anything, and less if its failures run long. This is a token-only calculation with index-wide cost figures, and it leaves out the larger term, human or orchestrator review time per attempt. With METR's estimate of 26 to 42 minutes of fix-up per agent PR, a single extra review cycle outweighs the token cost of a frontier run. The token math says pi-first is almost always cheap to try, while agy-first only pays when the task is clearly inside agy's range.

Escalation has costs beyond the wasted attempt. SWE-Router found that a strong model given a weak model's failed reasoning was "biased toward its mistakes," so escalated runs restart from the original prompt and the cheap turns become pure overhead ([arXiv 2607.00053](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00053v1)). Cascades lose money on tasks too hard for the cheap tier, with one function-level cascade on APPS-intro costing 1.6% more than no cascade at all ([arXiv 2405.15842](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15842v2)). A rule-based router in TwinRouterBench cost **$172.56 against $54.73 for always-Opus** on the same 100 SWE-bench tasks and solved one fewer ([arXiv 2605.18859](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18859v1)). Context design can matter as much as tier choice. Unblocked reported that curated context alone cut tokens by 42% and tool calls by 64% for the same model ([Unblocked](https://getunblocked.com/blog/model-routing-coding-agents/)), and Anthropic reports multi-agent systems use about 15 times the tokens of chat, with token use explaining 80% of research-eval variance ([Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)).

## Routing studies cut cost about half but rarely beat the strongest model

The headline savings from classic routing papers do not transfer to coding agents. FrugalGPT reported up to 98% savings and RouteLLM over 85% on MT-Bench at 95% of GPT-4 quality, but on chat, QA and math data ([arXiv 2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176); [arXiv 2406.18665](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665)). The 2026 agentic-coding results are smaller and more conditional. **A trained router in TwinRouterBench resolved 75 of 100 SWE-bench Verified tasks for $25.66 against 74 for always-Opus 4.6 at $54.73, a 53% cut** ([arXiv 2605.18859](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18859v1)). SuperScout solved 159 of 266 SWE-bench Pro Python tasks against 158 for Opus 4.6 alone at **$0.230 against $1.274 per solve**, but an ablation that always picked the cheapest fixer tied it, so the gain came from a 7B scout's sandbox-verified handoff, not from routing ([arXiv 2608.04804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04804)). Not Diamond claims Opus 4.8 xhigh quality at 39% lower cost from per-step routing inside Claude Code, without published method ([Not Diamond](https://www.notdiamond.ai/blog/not-diamond-code-intelligent-model-routing-for-coding-agents)).

The SuperScout authors also replayed routers over public per-task results and found **solve sets strongly nested, with containment of 0.91 to 0.94 on SWE-bench Verified and Multilingual and 0.77 on Pro**. No learned router beat always picking the strongest model on solve rate. Routing for quality has little headroom, and routing for cost only needs to predict when the cheaper model is enough. Predicting that from the task text is hard. On CodeRouterBench's out-of-distribution agentic split, lightweight prompt classifiers fell to 9 to 21% average performance, below random at 31% ([arXiv 2606.22902](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22902v2)). Routers that read a few turns of a cheap model's exploration did better, raising Route-AUC from 0.627 to 0.780, still well short of reliable ([arXiv 2607.00053](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00053v1)). Opus 4.6 prompted as a per-step router marked only 7 of 147 verified-hard steps as hard and failed all 40 trajectories ([arXiv 2605.18859](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18859v1)). Cascade depth adds little. Across five benchmarks and eight models, two-model cascades beat single-model selection by 2.1 to 8.2 points, but **adding a third model did not raise accuracy in 118 of 135 comparisons** ([arXiv 2605.06350](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06350)).

Splitting planning from execution has the most consistent support. Aider's architect/editor pairs beat the architect's solo score in every tested pair, for example o1-preview with o1-mini at 85.0% against 79.7% ([Aider](https://aider.chat/2024/09/26/architect.html)). Anthropic's advisor tool, where Sonnet or Haiku calls Opus mid-task, gave **Sonnet 4.6 +2.7 points on SWE-bench Multilingual at 11.9% lower cost per task than Sonnet alone**, and doubled Haiku's BrowseComp score, though run conditions differed and no comparison against Opus solo was published ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/the-advisor-strategy)). Vendors have productized the same idea. Claude Code's `opusplan` plans on Opus and executes on Sonnet, its subagent docs pitch Haiku for cost control, and Gemini CLI and Cursor route simple requests to cheaper models by default ([Claude Code model config](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/model-config); [Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/gemini-3-flash-is-now-available-in-gemini-cli/); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/help/models-and-usage/cursor-router)). None of these results shows a split beating the strong model run solo under matched conditions on coding. Their value is holding most of the quality for less money.

## Practical routing for Codex, agy and pi

The draft swarm rubric in `plans/swarm-orchestration.md` already routes by task type, picks the lowest tier that fits, picks the higher tier when torn, and escalates after two failures. The evidence supports most of it and suggests five adjustments. This guidance assumes Codex runs a current flagship (GPT-6.1 Sol or Astra), agy runs a Sonnet-class model and pi runs a Haiku-class model. The notes do not establish which models agy and pi run, and Codex itself offers Luna, so the configured model should be checked before trusting any tier label.

| Tier | Send | Do not send | Required controls |
|---|---|---|---|
| pi (low) | Mechanical edits, renames, pattern-following boilerplate, lookups, summaries, running checks, any short task with an existing test, type or lint check | Tasks without an automated check, long exploration of large repos, anything needing a design choice | Read-only tests, orchestrator reruns checks, small context handoff |
| agy (mid) | Bounded features with a written spec and acceptance tests, UI work, tests for existing behavior, moderate refactors in one known area | Unclear specs, cross-module refactors, bugs with unknown cause | Spec or interfaces written first, premise check before editing, diff review |
| Codex (top) | Long multi-step work, cross-cutting refactors, hard debugging, unclear specs, writing specs and interfaces for lower tiers, final review of risky diffs | Mechanical edits sent "to be safe" | Scope limits against overreach, state-based verification |

First, route by horizon and checkability rather than category alone. The tier gap tracks how long and open-ended the task is, and weak models gain most from machine-checkable specs, so the deciding questions are whether the task fits in under an hour of human work and whether a test, type check or lint rule will judge it. A pi task without an automated check is a guess. Second, use Codex as the planner for lower tiers. The strongest numeric support in this evidence is for strong-plan, cheap-execute splits, and the Siemens result suggests a Codex-written interface or failing test does more for agy and pi output than any amount of review afterwards.

Third, change the escalation rule. Restart escalated work from the original assignment plus verified facts, never from the failed transcript. Allow a same-tier retry only for environment or transient failures, and escalate after the first reasoning failure such as a wrong premise, wrong-problem repair or looping, since those are the failures agents rarely recover from and nested solve sets mean a second attempt at the same tier rarely reaches new ground. Avoid routine pi-to-agy-to-Codex chains, because third-tier depth rarely helped accuracy, and when pi fails on difficulty rather than on a slip, consider going straight to Codex. Fourth, verify against state, not reports. Every tier overclaims, LLM judges barely beat chance at spotting false success, and pi-class models hardcode tests more often, so the orchestrator should rerun tests itself, inspect the diff and file list, make test files read-only for workers, and ask each worker to state its premise and plan before editing, where most failures begin. Fifth, treat security as a control problem. Model size barely changes security pass rates, so keep security-sensitive design on Codex if desired but add a scanner or explicit security review for every tier.

The mid tier deserves the closest watch. With per-task cost only about 1.7 times lower than the top tier, agy pays off on tasks it will usually get right, and the 5.5-generation benchmarks say that range is now wide. For assignments on the agy and Codex boundary, the rubric's "pick the higher tier when torn" rule matches the cost math. For assignments on the pi and agy boundary, trying pi first is cheap enough to favor the lower tier whenever an automated check exists.

## Conclusion

The question "can cheaper models match frontier models" has stopped having one answer. In the current generation the mid tier is a near-peer whose savings are modest, and the small tier is a cheap specialist whose savings are large but whose quality drops off sharply once a task needs many steps or judgment. That makes the small tier the real cost lever in a three-tier swarm and the mid tier mainly a latency and quota lever. It also moves the main risk away from token spend and toward review. A cheap model that fails loudly costs a few cents, while one that passes the tests with the wrong behavior costs reviewer time that no benchmark measures.

Three things would change this guidance. Per-tier review-time data on real codebases would replace the token-based break-even with a true total cost. Practitioner reports on the 5.5 generation would show whether Sonnet 5.5's benchmark parity holds on messy repos. And each model release can reorder the tiers, as GPT-5.6 Luna overtaking GPT-5.5 showed, so the swarm should log escalations and verification failures per tier and revisit the rubric whenever a CLI's default model changes.
